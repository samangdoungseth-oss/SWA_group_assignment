Dataset Overview

This project uses public Bluesky posts discussing the 2026 Nepal floods. Posts were collected using the atrrr package in R through the Bluesky API.

Four search queries were used:

  • Nepal flood
  • Nepal floods
  • Nepal flooding
  • Nepal glacier

Posts were collected from 26 August 2026 onwards, covering discussion from the beginning of the flood event through to the date of data collection.

The initial searches returned 1,584 records. After duplicate posts were removed using the unique Bluesky uri field, 1,385 unique posts remained.

Cleaning Post Text

The original post text was retained, while a separate clean_text variable was created for later text analysis. URLs, punctuation, numbers and excess whitespace were removed, and all text was converted to lowercase.

posts = posts %>%
  mutate(
    clean_text = text,

    # Remove URLs and web addresses

      clean_text,
      "https?://\\S+|www\\.\\S+|\\b\\S+\\.(com|org|net|au|co|io)(/\\S*)?",
      " "

    # Remove numbers
    clean_text = str_replace_all(clean_text, "[0-9]+", " "),

    # Remove punctuation
    clean_text = str_replace_all(clean_text, "[[:punct:]]", " "),

Hypothesis Testing

Research Question

The hypothesis testing section examines whether one characteristic of a post, the presence of an external link, is associated with its engagement.

The research question is:

Do Bluesky posts about the “Nepal Flood” with links have different engagement levels compared with posts without links?

Posts containing links may be used to share news reports, updates or other external information. Therefore, examining their engagement provides insight into how users interact with information-sharing posts during discussion of the Nepal flood.

The later clustering and network analyses focus on the content of the discussion, while this hypothesis test focuses on engagement with the posts.

Hypotheses

\(H_0\): Posts with links have equal engagement to posts without links.

\[\mu_{link} = \mu_{no\ link}\]

\(H_A\): Posts with links and posts without links do not have the same engagement level.

\[\mu_{link} \neq \mu_{no\ link}\]

Why This Test

A Welch two-sample t-test was selected because the response variable, total_engagement, is numerical, while has_link divides the posts into two independent groups: posts with links and posts without links.

A chi-squared test of independence was also considered. However, this would require converting the numerical engagement score into categories such as “high” and “low” engagement. Doing so would discard information contained in the original numerical variable. Therefore, a two-sample t-test provides a more appropriate comparison of the mean engagement scores between the two groups.

Method

Data Preparation

Firstly, a new dataframe is created to only store the text and total_engagement. Then a new column called has_link is added which contains the datatype of TRUE/FALSE. grepl() is used to check whether the text contains either https://, http:// or www.

posts_and_scores <- posts[, c("text", "total_engagement")]

posts_and_scores$has_link <- grepl(
  "https?://|www",
  posts_and_scores$text,
  ignore.case = TRUE
)

Posts that contain those queries were assigned to TRUE.

Group Size

The group size was checked using the table() function.

# count unique elements in has_link, which are T/F
table(posts_and_scores$has_link)
## 
## FALSE  TRUE 
##   808   529

There were 808 posts without links and 529 posts with links, providing enough data size for the groups comparison.

Descriptive Statistic

The mean engagement score was calculated separately for both groups.

# group by "has_link" and find the mean using "total_engagement"
aggregate(
  total_engagement ~ has_link,
  data = posts_and_scores,
  mean
)

Posts without links had a mean engagement score of 5.57, while posts with links had a mean engagement score of 5.00. The difference between the two is approximately 0.57 engagement points.

Although the descriptive result suggests slightly higher engagement among posts without links, a statistical test is required to determine whether the observed difference is significant.

Welch Two-Sample t-test

Welch two-sample t-test is used to compare the mean between the two groups.

t.test(
  total_engagement ~ has_link,
  data = posts_and_scores
)
## 
##  Welch Two Sample t-test

Result

The test provides a p-value of 0.66. Since it is greater than the significance level of 0.05, the null hypothesis was not rejected.

The 95% confidence interval of the difference in mean is approximately:

\[[-1.95, 3.08]\]

This interval includes zero which is also consistent with our observation earlier.

We can conclude that the statistical test found insufficient evidence to suggest that Bluesky posts with links have different engagement to posts without links within this collected data set.

TF-IDF Weighting

TF-IDF weighting was used to reduce the influence of terms that occur frequently across many posts while giving greater importance to terms that are more distinctive to particular documents.

Since the DTM had already been converted into a matrix, TF-IDF was calculated manually.

The term frequency was calculated using:

\[TF = \log(f + 1)\]

while inverse document frequency was calculated using:

\[IDF = \log\left(\frac{N}{df}\right)\]

The final TF-IDF matrix was then obtained by multiplying the TF and IDF values.

N <- nrow(dtm_matrix)

IDF <- log(N / colSums(dtm_matrix > 0))

TF <- log(dtm_matrix + 1)

weighted_tfidf <- TF %*% diag(IDF)

Normalisation

The TF-IDF vectors were normalised to unit length before clustering. This reduces the influence of document length so that longer posts do not dominate the distance calculation simply because they contain more words.

Each document vector was divided by its Euclidean length:

\[\hat{x} = \frac{x}{||x||}\]

norm_tfidf <- diag(
  1 / sqrt(rowSums(weighted_tfidf^2))
) %*% weighted_tfidf

A check was then performed to confirm that the Euclidean length of each document vector was approximately 1.

Selecting the Number of Clusters

K-means clustering requires the number of clusters, \(k\), to be selected beforehand. The within-cluster sum of squares (SSW) was calculated for values of \(k\) from 1 to 10.

A lower SSW indicates that posts are located closer to the centre of their assigned cluster. However, increasing the number of clusters will naturally reduce SSW, so the elbow method was used to identify a point where further increases in \(k\) produced smaller improvements.

set.seed(3020)

SSW <- numeric(10)

for (n in 1:10) {
  K <- kmeans(
    norm_tfidf,
    n,
    nstart = 30
  )

  SSW[n] <- K$tot.withinss
}

The resulting SSW values were plotted against the number of clusters.

plot(
  1:10,
  SSW,
  type = "b",
  xlab = "Number of Clusters",
  ylab = "Within Cluster sum of squares"
)

\(K = 7\) was selected because this was the first visible “elbow” in the plot above.

Clustering Results

The clustering analysis identified seven groups of posts discussing different aspects of the Nepal flood and related issues.

Cluster 4 was substantially larger than the other clusters, containing 812 posts, and appeared to represent broad discussion of the disaster, including missing people, affected communities and devastation.

Several smaller clusters represented more specific topics. Cluster 1 focused on landslides and casualties, while Clusters 2 and 6 contained discussion relating to glaciers, warming and climate change. Cluster 3 appeared to focus on climate justice and disaster funding.

The smallest clusters represented more specialised discussions. Cluster 5 contained terms relating to studies, Tibet and the wider crisis, while Cluster 7 appeared to involve information sharing and accounts discussing the event.

Overall, the clustering results suggest that although much of the Bluesky discussion consisted of general reporting and discussion of the disaster, several smaller groups of posts focused on specific environmental, social and climate-related issues.

Define nodes and edges, explain what edges represent and justify whether the graph is directed or undirected

The nodes in the graph represent the unique terms that are found throughout the document whiles edges are used to represent the connection between those words. If two nodes are linked by an edge it shows that they appear in the same document at least one. Weighted edges are are also used to outline the frequency of these connections. A higher weighted edge means that two unique words occur in the same document multiple times.

Furthermore, the graph is undirected. This is determined by examining the adjacency matrix. The matrix is symmetrical along its diagonal which means the weight from A to B and the weight from B to A is the exact same. Therefore the graph is undirected.

The API that was used to collect data also comes with limitations that can potentially skew results. This means that the posts available to be retrieved may represent a specific demographic of people rather than representing the views of the whole population.

This has the potential to affect our results by producing nodes that might be central to the data we collected but might not be central to the overall topic we are trying to understand.

Filtering and Network construction decisions

When preprocessing the data, it is important to use filtering to remove all punctuation, numbers and empty posts to ensure that only terms that can be used remain. In addition, stemming has been used to reduce words into their base forms which helps improve node frequency and node centrality. Despite applying these two processes, there were still over 3735 unique terms in the adjacency matrix which is too large to produce a graph. To overcome this issue, TF-IDF was used to filter out the less influential nodes. After applying weights to each term, they were then ordered in descending order and the top 500 terms were taken to create a new adjacency matrix. The new matrix was now small enough to produce a graph that is more manageable.

When creating the graph, there were still a lot of terms which made it hard to read and interpret the information. As a result, a subgraph was created that would allow the viewer to focus in on the most important terms in the data. By creating an ego network of order 1, the graph was able to display only immediate connections to the most central node which is “climate”. This process significantly reduced the number of nodes shown but was still very difficult to read. To ensure a clean and readable subgraph, we chose to display the top 15 nodes with the highest degree by using the induce_subgraph function. This resulted in an extremely readable graph that emphasises the connectedness of the top 15 most significant nodes to the topic of the Nepal flood.

The investigation into the Nepal floods through text mining and the construction of graphs has helped uncover key terms that are associated with the events. These two methods were chosen as they provide information on two separate aspects which results in a strong holistic understanding of how the terms relate to each other. The degree centrality is used to determine which node has the most edges. This can be interpreted as which term appears most frequently in a document with other given terms. The eigenvector was used to build on this by checking if a node is influential because it is connected to more terms or if it is influential because it’s connected to other nodes that have a high degree. The eigenvector centrality therefore provides a deeper layer of insight by highlighting terms that sit within influential neighbourhoods of the network rather than exlusively looking at each term’s frequency. Together, these two measures provide a clear understanding of how terms relate to one another as well as which concepts have the most impact in shaping the discussion around the Nepal floods.
